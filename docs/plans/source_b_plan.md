# Source B: BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (Industrial Core)

## Context

Sources A (Wikipedia embeddings), C (FRED velocity), D (BTS FAF5 trade flows), and F (USDA typology) are complete. Source B (BLS QCEW) was flagged in `source_d_plan.md` as "not started yet ... despite an earlier session flagging it as next." No `ingest_source_b.py` or `source_b_plan.md` existed until now.

**Source B = "BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (Industrial Core)"**: pull pre-calculated Location Quotients (LQ) across the 20 primary 2-digit NAICS sectors for all counties, via BLS's QCEW Open Data Access. Conceptually this is the "Industrial Core" pillar of `E_macro`, per `docs/E_macro_extendedProposal.pdf` — it identifies which industries actually drive a county's economy at scale-invariant resolution (a LQ of 2.0 means twice the national-average concentration of jobs in that sector, regardless of the county's absolute size). Paired with Source C's velocity, it distinguishes *what kind* of growth or decline a county is experiencing (e.g., tech-driven acceleration vs. manufacturing-driven acceleration) rather than just its direction.

`docs/macro_pre_scoping_spec.pdf` flags one real risk up front: BLS suppresses LQ cells in counties where small employer counts could expose individual company operations, and proposes an Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) matrix-completion fix — then immediately flags its own proposed fix as flawed ("executing this formulation independently per county ignores multi-county column constraints... introducing severe conservation-of-mass errors"). That's the central open design question for this source, resolved empirically below rather than picked a priori (same approach as Source D's vectorization decision).

## Risks (in priority order)

### Risk 1: access mechanism — resolved, mirrors Source D's landing-page/data-host split
`www.bls.gov` (the docs/landing pages) returns a bare **403** to plain `curl`, same shape as Source D's `bts.gov` block. But the actual QCEW Open Data Access files live on **`data.bls.gov`**, which has **no bot protection** — confirmed via plain `curl` and Python `requests` (200 OK, no special headers, no TLS quirk — unlike Source D, `requests` works fine here, no `curl` subprocess workaround needed).

### Risk 2: data suppression / LQ estimation — real, but scoped smaller than the spec implied
Confirmed real and sector-dependent (see Phase 0 findings below): suppression at the target NAICS-2-digit / private-ownership / county level ranges from **~14% (Finance, NAICS 52) to ~46% (Agriculture, NAICS 11)** of county rows nationally — not the flat 60% a general QCEW suppression search suggests (that figure blends in much finer NAICS levels, which we're not using). The spec's own proposed IPF fix is not attempted here as a starting point given its self-identified flaw; Phase 1b will compare simpler alternatives empirically (null-passthrough, state-level LQ fallback, or a constrained multi-county solve) before picking one.

## Phases

### Phase 0: Reconnaissance — complete (2026-07-14)
1. Confirmed `data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/{year}/{qtr}/{industry|area}/{code}.csv` as the real, ungated access pattern (both "by area" and "by industry" slices exist).
2. **Key efficiency finding: use industry-slices, not area-slices.** An area-slice (`.../area/{fips}.csv`) returns one county's full NAICS breakdown (~20-40 rows). An industry-slice (`.../industry/{naics2}.csv`) returns **one NAICS sector's rows for every county nationally in a single file** (~3,200 rows). Since the target is exactly "20 sectors × all counties," downloading the **20 industry-slice files** (one per 2-digit NAICS code) is ~20 requests total instead of ~3,142 — the same shape as Source D's discovery that BTS had already pre-solved the combinatorial problem, just found via the industry axis instead of area.
3. Confirmed schema via direct download (San Francisco County 06075, Loving County TX 48301, Petroleum County MT 30069, national NAICS-11 and NAICS-52 industry slices, all 2023 Q1):
   - Columns: `area_fips, own_code, industry_code, agglvl_code, size_code, year, qtr, disclosure_code, qtrly_estabs, month{1,2,3}_emplvl, total_qtrly_wages, taxable_qtrly_wages, qtrly_contributions, avg_wkly_wage, lq_disclosure_code, lq_qtrly_estabs, lq_month{1,2,3}_emplvl, lq_total_qtrly_wages, lq_taxable_qtrly_wages, lq_qtrly_contributions, lq_avg_wkly_wage, oty_*` (over-the-year change columns, not needed here).
   - **`own_code="5"` (Private) + `agglvl_code="74"`** is the target slice: exactly the 20 codes the proposal describes — `11, 21, 22, 23, 31-33, 42, 44-45, 48-49, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 61, 62, 71, 72, 81, 99` (`99` = unclassified; no `92`/government code appears here because this is the *private-ownership* view — see open question below).
   - **Disclosure mechanism confirmed exactly as the spec worried:** when `disclosure_code="N"`, the corresponding `lq_*` fields are `0`/blank rather than the true value — a suppressed cell is indistinguishable from a genuine zero unless the disclosure code is carried alongside the LQ value. Any ingestion must keep `disclosure_code`/`lq_disclosure_code` as a null-marker, not just read `lq_month1_emplvl` at face value.
4. **Open question carried to Phase 1:** the proposal's "Location Quotients for every level of NAICS" plus "Industrial Core" framing suggests we may want a *total* (all-ownership) view, not just Private. `own_code="0"` exists but doesn't appear to carry a 2-digit NAICS breakdown in the same file (only a single `industry_code="10"` total row) — government employment is likely only broken out at the supersector level, not full NAICS-2-digit, in QCEW's own_code=0 slices. Confirm this in Phase 1 before deciding whether Source B ships Private-only LQs (simpler, matches the "Industrial Core" private-sector framing most literally) or attempts a Private+Government composite.

### Phase 1: Credentials & dependencies — complete (2026-07-14)
- **No API key required** (confirmed — plain `requests`, no auth, no rate-limit response observed across ~10 test requests).
- **No new dependencies** needed beyond what's already in `pyproject.toml` (`requests`, `pandas`, `pyarrow`).
- **Reference period: 2025 Q4.** Confirmed live: `2025/4` returns real data (200, non-empty), `2026/1` returns `404` — consistent with BLS's own published schedule (Q1 2026 county data releases 2026-08-28, per the QCEW release calendar found in Phase 0). 2025 Q4 is the most recent fully-published quarter as of this writing.
- **Own_code scope decision: ship Private ownership only (`own_code="5"`).** Checked federal/state/local government rows (`own_code` 1/2/3) directly on the San Francisco County file: they do have `agglvl_code="74"` (2-digit NAICS) rows, but only for a sparse subset of sectors each (e.g., federal government shows activity in ~8 of the 20 codes, state in ~5, local in ~9 — government employment naturally doesn't spread across all NAICS sectors the way private employment does). `own_code="0"` (the actual all-ownership total) does **not** carry a 2-digit breakdown at all in this file — only a single `industry_code="10"` grand-total row. So there is no pre-computed "true" all-ownership LQ to consume; building one would mean recomputing LQ from raw employment levels summed across four ownership codes ourselves, rather than reading BLS's own `lq_*` columns.
  - **Reasoning for Private-only:** the proposal's own stated purpose for this source — distinguishing "specialized white-collar tech hubs" from "blue-collar manufacturing centers" — is a private-industrial-structure question. Government employment concentration (e.g., a state capital's NAICS-92 spike) reflects facility placement, not industrial specialization, and would dilute the private signal without adding anything the proposal actually asked for. Private-only is also the option BLS ships pre-computed and already validated in Phase 0 (all 20 codes present, clean schema) — lower risk, less engineering, and a closer literal match to the proposal's own framing. This is a judgment call, not a forced conclusion — flagging it explicitly here rather than picking silently, and it's the kind of thing to revisit if Phase 3/4 analysis finds the private-only signal is missing something a reviewer would expect.

### Phase 1b: Suppression-handling comparison (resolves Risk 2) — complete (2026-07-14)

**Access detour, not anticipated in Phase 0:** the 3 combined 2-digit codes (`31-33`, `44-45`, `48-49`) 404 as individual `industry`-slice URLs — tried the literal hyphenated code, several no-hyphen variants (`3133`, `4445`, `4849`), and the standalone halves (`31`/`32`/`33`, `44`/`45`, `48`/`49`); none resolve (one guess, `3133`, returned 200 but turned out to be an unrelated real 4-digit NAICS code, not the combined sector — a red herring, caught by checking its `industry_code` field rather than trusting the status code alone). Fell back to BLS's bulk **`data.bls.gov/cew/data/files/{year}/csv/{year}_qtrly_singlefile.zip`** (287MB compressed, 2.2GB uncompressed, all 4 quarters/all areas/all ownerships/all industries/all size classes in one file) and filtered locally — this also simplifies Phase 2, since it's one download instead of managing 20 industry-slice requests plus edge cases for the 3 that don't resolve.

Filtered to `qtr="4"`, `own_code="5"` (Private, per Phase 1's decision): **60,985 county rows** (`agglvl_code="74"`) across exactly the 20 target codes, **1,051 state rows** (`agglvl_code="54"`) — confirms Phase 1's own_code scope and Phase 0's 20-code list against the full national dataset, not just the two spot-checked sectors.

**Real national suppression rate: 30.0%** of county×sector cells (own_code=5, agglvl=74, 2025 Q4) — lower than the general "~60%" QCEW figure (which blends much finer NAICS levels) but not negligible. Sector range is wide: **Retail Trade (44-45) only 3.1% suppressed** (present almost everywhere) vs. **Mining (21) 59.0%** and **Agriculture (11) 52.9%** (naturally concentrated in few counties). Suppressed cells have a median of 5 establishments vs. 40 for disclosed cells — confirms suppression tracks genuinely small local presence, not a data-quality artifact.

Compared candidate strategies:

| Option | What it is | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Null-passthrough** | Keep `lq_*` null wherever `disclosure_code="N"`. | Simplest, no fabricated numbers, but leaves real gaps in 30% of cells on average (up to 59% for some sectors). |
| **B. State-level LQ fallback** | Substitute the state's LQ for that sector where suppressed. | **Tested against 42,704 held-out disclosed county cells: MAE=0.786, Pearson r=0.334 vs. the true county LQ — barely better than just using the global mean LQ everywhere (MAE=0.947).** Weaker than assumed when this option was first proposed. |
| **C. Constrained multi-county solve** | The spec's corrected IPF idea — respects state-level totals across all counties in a state simultaneously. | Median within-state county-LQ std dev (0.519) is 59% as large as the between-state std dev (0.880) — most of the true variation lives *within* a state, which is exactly the part any state-margin-constrained solve (IPF included) has the least power to recover. Tested a lighter, correctly-specified proxy for this idea instead of building full IPF (see below) — result made building the full version not worth it. |

**C-lite test:** rather than full IPF, allocated each suppressed county's sector employment proportionally to that county's share of total (all-industry) private employment within its state — a single closed-form calculation that exactly satisfies the state total by construction (unlike the spec's flawed per-county-independent IPF), using QCEW's own disclosed county/state employment totals (no new data source). **Result: MAE=0.786, r=0.340 on the same 42,554 held-out disclosed cells — statistically indistinguishable from Option B's MAE=0.786, r=0.334.** A county's overall economic size doesn't predict its *sector-specific* concentration any better than its state's average does. Since this simpler, cheaper proxy for "respect state totals" produced no improvement over a flat state substitute, the full multi-county IPF solve (which rests on the same state-margin information, just distributed more carefully) is very unlikely to do meaningfully better — not worth building for the marginal-at-best payoff, mirroring Source D's precedent of dropping a complex option once a cheaper test showed no real signal (Option B / distance-reach there).

**Decision: ship Option A (null-passthrough with the `disclosure_code`/`lq_disclosure_code` flags preserved as explicit nullability markers).** Neither state-fallback nor a proportional-allocation proxy for the spec's IPF idea meaningfully recovers suppressed cells beyond a weak, near-global-mean-level signal (r≈0.33-0.34 either way) — shipping either as a substitute would read as more precise than it is. A null with an honest disclosure flag is preferable to a number that's barely better than guessing the national average.

### Phase 2: `scripts/ingest_source_b.py` — complete (2026-07-14)
Mirrors Sources A/C/D/F's established architecture (module docstring, `configure_logging`/`main` entrypoint, isolated download/transform/storage sections):
- Downloads BLS's bulk `{year}_qtrly_singlefile.zip` (per Phase 1b's access-detour finding) via plain `requests`, streamed to a temp file; stream-filters to `qtr="4"`, `own_code="5"`, `agglvl_code="74"` in 500k-row chunks to avoid holding the full 2.2GB CSV in memory.
- Pivots from long (one row per county×sector) to one row per county: 20 `lq_emp_{naics2}` columns + 20 matching `disclosure_{naics2}` boolean flag columns.
- **Real bug caught during verification:** BLS reports a suppressed cell's `lq_month3_emplvl` as a literal `0`, not blank — `pd.to_numeric` alone doesn't null it out. The first draft left suppressed cells at `0.0` instead of null, silently reintroducing the exact false-zero problem Phase 0 flagged. Fixed by explicitly nulling `lq_emp_{naics2}` wherever `disclosure_{naics2}` is `True`, keyed off the disclosure flag rather than the raw value.
- The wide pivot also naturally distinguishes two different kinds of missingness: `disclosure_{naics2}=True` (BLS suppressed the cell) vs. `disclosure_{naics2}=<NA>` (no row at all for that county×sector — e.g. a sector with no local presence). Both leave `lq_emp_{naics2}` null, but the flag tells them apart.
- Joins to `county_crosswalk.parquet` on `area_fips`/`fips_code` (direct FIPS match, no new crosswalk needed).
- Output: `data/source_b_qcew.parquet` — **3,143 rows, 42 columns** (`fips_code`, `county_name`, 20 LQ columns, 20 disclosure-flag columns). 3,143 of 3,144 crosswalk counties matched; the one gap is Kalawao County, HI (`15005`, population ~90), which has no QCEW private-sector county-level row at all.
- Verified against the cached Phase 1b bulk file (same 2025 Q4 data, no re-download needed): national suppression rate reproduced exactly at 30.0% (n=58,635 non-null cells), zero suppressed cells left with a non-null LQ after the fix. Spot-checked San Francisco County (`06075`, fully disclosed — all 20 raw LQ values match the output exactly) and Loving County, TX (`48301`, 19 of 20 sectors either suppressed or absent — only NAICS 31-33 disclosed at LQ=1.95, matches exactly).

### Phase 3: Analysis scripts (mirroring the per-concern script split) — complete (2026-07-14)
- `scripts/analyze_source_b_industry_mix.py` — collapses each county's 20 sector LQs to a single `dominant_naics2`/`dominant_industry`/`dominant_lq` (highest-LQ sector and its magnitude), mirroring Source F's one-hot `dependence_label` collapse but over continuous LQ values instead of binary flags. **Real bug caught during testing:** pandas' `idxmax(skipna=True)` raises `ValueError` on an all-NA row — a handful of counties have every sector suppressed or absent — rather than returning null. Fixed via `lq.fillna(float("-inf")).idxmax(axis=1)` then masking back to null with `.where(has_any_value)`. Results: Manufacturing is the most common dominant sector (695 counties, 22.1%), followed by Agriculture (489, 15.6%) and Mining (367, 11.7%); 10 counties (0.3%) have no disclosed/present sector at all. Most specialized single county: Eureka County, NV (dominant_lq=248.09, Mining) — a real, extreme single-industry mining county, not an artifact.
- `scripts/analyze_source_b_source_c_correlation.py` — cross-validates both the `dominant_lq` magnitude and each of the 20 individual sector LQs against Source C's `gdp_velocity_pct`/`unemployment_velocity`, analogous to `analyze_source_d_source_c_correlation.py`. Results: `dominant_lq` itself is a weak predictor (r=-0.095 vs. unemployment velocity, r=0.046 vs. GDP velocity %, both significant at p<0.02 but small in magnitude — n=3,080 after dropping counties with no Source C GDP series). Per-sector correlations are more informative: Real Estate & Rental & Leasing LQ correlates strongest with GDP velocity (r=0.206, n=2,396), followed by Wholesale Trade (r=-0.157, n=2,307) and Construction (r=0.154, n=2,435) — a real signal that *which* sector dominates matters more than *how strongly* one sector dominates. **Real methodological issue caught by code review and fixed:** scanning all 20 sectors and reporting only the top 3 by raw \|r\| inflates apparent significance (expected ~1 of 20 crosses p<0.05 by chance) and different sectors have very different disclosed-county counts (BLS suppression targets small-establishment counties non-randomly, so a thinly-disclosed sector's correlation is over a biased subsample). Fixed by adding a Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (`stats_utils.benjamini_hochberg`, implemented directly rather than adding a `statsmodels` dependency) reported alongside the raw p-value, and a `MIN_SECTOR_DISCLOSED_COUNT=100` floor before a sector is eligible for the top-N ranking. All three reported sectors survive correction (q=0.0067, well above the n floor at 2,300+ each) — a real signal, not a multiple-comparisons artifact.
- `scripts/visualize_source_b.py` — two US bubble maps: dominant NAICS-2 sector (categorical, 20-color qualitative palette) and specialization magnitude (continuous, dominant LQ), mirroring `visualize_source_f.py`'s two-map structure.
- `scripts/generate_source_b_insights.py` — writes `analysis-output/source_b_stats.json`, three static PNG figures, and `analysis-output/figures/source-b-numeric-summary.md`, mirroring `generate_source_f_insights.py`.

All 4 scripts ran end-to-end without errors; outputs verified in `outputs/source_b_*.csv`/`.html` and `analysis-output/figures/source-b-*`.
